## Translated text

On a terrain extending up to 1,000 meters in depth and several hundred meters in width, with its lateral boundaries established, a patrol of *reds* is positioned. One lies, kneels, or stands closer, another farther away; in one spot stands a single scout, in another two together, and so on. Distances of 100—200—300 meters, etc., up to 1,000 meters have previously been measured and noted by both patrol leaders, along with the terrain features located at these distances.

At the observation point stands the patrol leader of the *whites*, who, upon the signal to begin the exercise, raises a flag upward. The *reds* remain motionless so as not to reveal their positions (hiding is not permitted), while the *whites* approach their patrol leader from concealment, and each individually searches the terrain for the *reds* (the number of whom should be known to them). If the *whites* cannot locate one of the *reds*, the patrol leader narrows the observation area to assist them; if even then no one finds him, the flag is moved left and right, and at this signal, the *reds* begin to move, making them easier to spot.

After all have been located, their distances are estimated. Each records their estimates in their own card, in the order prescribed by the patrol leader. Then, the actual distances are provided to verify any errors.

The *whites* return to their concealment, the flag is lowered, and the *reds* change their positions. When they are ready, they signal with a whistle. The flag is then raised again. The *whites* emerge once more and search the terrain again, then estimate the distances. This is repeated three times, after which both patrols switch roles.

If the exercise is competitive, upon completion, the percentage of errors for both patrols is calculated to determine the winner.

This exercise can be varied by setting a time limit for observation (e.g., up to 5 minutes), during which each individual records their observations, noting precisely where and in what position the sought scout is located. Then, the notes are collected to determine who discovered the most scouts.
