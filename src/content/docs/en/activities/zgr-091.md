This game has gained enormous popularity in our country since the early years of the current century. Today, it is the most beloved game among male youth, to the detriment of other games of no lesser value. It is suitable for strong and healthy students of higher grades in secondary schools. The shorter boundaries are called the goal lines, and the longer ones the touch lines. The goal and touch lines must form right angles. At these corners, flags with poles no shorter than one and a half metres should be planted. A halfway line, the 'centre line,' is drawn across the middle of the field. The centre of the field must be clearly marked, and from it, a circle with a radius of 9 metres is drawn. (Lines should not be sunken but lie flush with the field. The flagpole must not be pointed at the top.) The goal consists of two posts driven vertically into the goal line, equidistant from the corners of the field and 7,30 metres apart; a crossbar is placed on them at a height of 2,40 metres from the ground. The width of the posts and crossbar must not exceed 12 cm.

Technical Instructions for Correct Kicking
The beginner will learn the rules of kicking in preparatory games 87—90. The only way of kicking that every novice knows is the toe kick. However, this method plays a minor role in football; it can send the ball far, but due to the small striking surface, it is very difficult to give it the desired direction. To increase its accuracy, players' boots have transversely cut toes. Nevertheless, in games 87 and 88, only the centre forward will use it to send the ball far, and in games 90 and 91, the goalkeeper and defenders will use it for the same purpose. The foot should strike slightly below the centre of the ball, and the axis of the foot should be aligned with the direction in which we want the ball to travel. Far greater use is made of striking with larger surfaces of the foot. The outer side of the foot is most often used when we want to pass the ball to a teammate to the side. However, it is also often used to score a goal in this way. An easier, more reliable, and stronger method is kicking with the inner side of the foot. However, to deceive the opponent, good control of the outer side is very valuable. It is also essential to acquire skill in striking with the instep. Most often, the foot is strongly extended in this case. We do this when we want to kick the ball strongly straight ahead in a strictly defined direction; thus, most shots on goal look like this. But passing the ball to a teammate to the side over a greater distance is also usually done with the instep, not to mention passing forward. If the ball is approaching a player in flight above the knees, and they want to pass it behind to one of their teammates, they often use a kick with a bent foot: the ball flies over the player's head to the back. If we want to pass a rolling or low-flying ball to the back, we use the heel for this. This also includes passing the ball from the ground to the hands of one's own goalkeeper (often used at a goal kick). In this case, the player lightly 'lifts' the ball by sliding the tip of the foot under it, avoiding any striking: this is not a kick but rather a throw with the foot. In all these kicking methods, two details must be carefully observed: Firstly, it is essential to train both the right and left foot equally, for one can never predict which of them the ball will present itself in a favourable position for kicking. Secondly, one must be able to kick the ball not only from the ground when it is stationary, as stopping it wastes too much valuable time and gives an advantage to the opponent. One must be able to give the ball the desired direction immediately, regardless of whether it is rolling towards us slowly or quickly, flying in the air, or bouncing. Attention should be paid to this even in the earliest preparatory games (87 and 88). Sometimes, however, it is worth stopping the ball: for example, if the opponent is far away, or if we want to lure them towards us and then pass the ball in an unexpected direction. When the ball is coming low, we face the direction of its travel and stand on tiptoes in a crouch so that the ball can only rebound a short distance in front of us. A higher-flying ball is 'killed' (i.e., brought down to the ground) by a light tap with the sole from above, or by a nudge with the knee, thigh, or body directed diagonally downward. In all body rebounds, the beginner should get used to keeping their arms close to the body, as even touching the ball with them does not infringe rule 9. All players (except the goalkeeper) must remember that a ball flying at a certain height can only be effectively rebounded with the head. These rebounds also have enormous application. Sometimes (especially at a corner kick or a high pass from the wing), goals are scored with them; very often, the ball is passed to teammates to the side, forward, or back, etc. The rebound should be made with the surface of the head facing the direction in which the ball is to fly—and add to it the appropriate movement of the neck, and if greater force is needed, jump in the direction of the intended flight of the ball. Dribbling, or 'the wheelbarrow,' is overused by beginner players (see further tactical instructions). But even an experienced player resorts to it sometimes (e.g., a winger when their wing is not threatened, etc.). Then, while running, they push rather than kick the ball alternately with the right and left foot so that it never moves away from their feet. However, as soon as they see themselves threatened, they should immediately stop dribbling and pass the ball to an unthreatened teammate. Stopping an opponent is one of the more difficult tasks for a player, given the strong temptation to infringe the rules. If an opponent is preparing to kick, we can relatively easily knock them over or unbalance them with a light body check and 'take' the ball (i.e., kick it towards our own team). For when they are standing on one leg, their balance is worse than that of the attacker standing on both. If an opponent is dribbling the ball, we can stop it with our foot. Even if the dribbler falls as a result, there is no infringement of the rules, as we aimed at the ball, not the player. In both cases, the player's fall is usually light and without any consequences. However, one must avoid not only tripping but also leaning in front of or (when retreating) behind the player, kicking them, hitting them with the knee, or stopping them with the hand; finally, any charging from behind, unless the player is deliberately obstructing us while facing their own goal. For any such infringements, an alert and conscientious referee will penalise severely—often even by sending the player off without warning. The technique of the goalkeeper's play will be discussed in the next chapter.

Tactical Instructions
The tactics prevailing in this game have undergone fundamental and very characteristic changes over time. Initially, individual play predominated. A skilful player would take possession of the ball, dribble it near the opponents' goal, and, to the applause of the crowds, often score a goal themselves, having successfully evaded the defenders. However, as soon as clubs appeared that abandoned this tactic in favour of collective play, where the ball is passed from player to player and all cooperate for a common goal, individual play began to suffer defeat after defeat and quickly disappeared. At the same time, however, the value of the game itself as an educational tool increased enormously. In addition to the division of labour provided for in the rules themselves, which designate a goalkeeper and grant them the privilege of using their hands, experience has taught that it is beneficial to divide the rest of the team into three lines. The first consists of five attacking players (namely: the centre forward, right and left wings, and two inside forwards between them). The second consists of three midfield players (centre half, right half, and left half); the third consists of two full-backs, right and left. The full-backs, together with the goalkeeper, form the defence; the midfield, as its name suggests, assists either the attack or the defence as needed. Assigning players to these categories according to their abilities is an important task for the team captain ('mother'). However, it would be a mistake to establish this division too rigidly. On the contrary, changes from time to time are necessary, both for the all-round development of the players and for practical purposes, and often during the game, a player who has been injured must be replaced by someone who usually plays in a different position. The attack should consist of light, agile, bold, and decisive players. All of them must master the art of quickly and accurately passing the ball to the side; all, and especially the wings, should be able to dribble when necessary. Finally, for all, and particularly the three central players, the ability to shoot accurately at goal from any position in which the ball may present itself at an opportune moment is an essential condition. For example, if the white team starts the game (as in the illustration on page 151), the centre forward moves the ball diagonally forward a little more than the distance prescribed by the rules (equal to its circumference); one of the inside forwards runs up and passes the ball to the winger, who, being least threatened by opponents, dribbles it straight ahead. The other attacking players run, maintaining their original distances from each other but always slightly behind the winger leading the ball (to avoid being offside in case of a pass from the wing near the goal). At a distance from which an effective shot can already be considered (or earlier if threatened with losing the ball), they pass to the centre, and then the three central players, still running forward, pass the ball to each other until one of them spots a moment when part of the goal is unguarded and kicks the ball into it. (Beginners are prone to the fatal mistake of shooting into the goalkeeper's hands instead of into a corner they cannot reach.) If the goalkeeper deflects the ball a short distance, shots must be repeated until successful. If, however, the red defence manages to pass the ball to their attack, the white attack retreats beyond the centre line, but only far enough to more easily regain the ball (passed by their midfield or full-backs) and renew the attack. However, attacking players should never interfere with the defence. Throughout the game, the attack runs spread across the width of the field, and each strictly adheres to their position so that their teammates, without looking, know where to pass the ball. Only near the opponents' goal does the attacking line somewhat converge. The midfield consists of experienced players, familiar with both attacking methods (passing to the side and forward, long-range shots at goal) and defence (stopping and taking the ball, etc.), but above all, the most enduring runners. This trio is in constant motion throughout the game, assisting either the attack or the defence. The midfield must always be near the ball. When the attacking line is leading the attack, the midfield's task is to run behind the attack at a distance of several metres and pass the ball back to them immediately if the opponent knocks it away. Near the goal, the midfield must also often shoot, especially when the attacking players are offside. When the opponents are attacking, the first line of defence is again the midfield. If they do not manage to knock the ball away immediately, the midfield players do not give up and constantly harass the attackers, in later stages of defence coordinating their efforts purposefully with the play of both full-backs. They always remember to kick the ball taken from the opponent as quickly as possible to their own attack and enable a new attack for their team. The midfield moves across the field, maintaining the players' positions relative to each other as in the illustration (page 151). When attacking opponents pass the ball to the side, the midfield, together with the full-backs, can interfere with each of them in passing and receiving the ball. When an opponent is dribbling the ball, it is best to take it from them in pairs, with one player charging the dribbler and the other dealing with the ball. Midfield players usually take throw-ins from the touch line and free kicks when the referee awards them far from the goal. The full-backs consist of strong players capable of effectively stopping an attacker and rebounding the ball from any position to a significant distance (if possible, to their own attacking line). However, they should also be skilled in accurate passing (to the other full-back or a midfield player), which sometimes serves their team better than a showy 'high kick,' which takes a lot of time and is less accurate. The full-backs must work well together, with the goalkeeper and the midfield, so that together they form a truly cohesive defence. One of the full-backs should never move too far from the goal and, if necessary (when the goalkeeper runs forward), even temporarily stand in it. However, the other can beneficially stay further away, especially knowing how to move forward when this can put the opposing attackers offside. Neither full-back should, under any circumstances, cling to the goal and obstruct the goalkeeper's view of the ball. The goalkeeper occupies the most difficult position in the entire team. They should be tall to be able to rebound high shots; extremely agile and nimble to never lose a fraction of a second in which they can still run to a threatened corner of the goal; moreover, brave and resilient, as well as strong, to endure rough charges from opponents. They must also be able to accurately judge the direction and speed of the projectile. The goalkeeper should use their privilege of using their hands whenever time permits and only kick or stop the ball with their feet as a last resort. If time allows, the ball should be caught rather than rebounded. The best way to save the goal for a longer time is to toss the caught ball and kick it in flight towards the wings. If the attackers interfere, the ball is thrown with the hands. A high shot that cannot be caught can sometimes still be rebounded with the fist (or more reliably with both fists). If a shot goes just under the crossbar, the goal can be saved by lightly tapping the ball upwards: the ball flies over the crossbar, and the referee awards a corner kick, which, however, is not always dangerous for the goal. A low shot into the corner of the goal can be repelled by a lightning-fast dive to the ground. The goalkeeper carefully observes the movements of the forwards and tries to anticipate the direction of the shot in advance. Only exceptionally and briefly may the goalkeeper leave the immediate vicinity of the goal. Their usual place is at 1= 1 ½/9 metres in front of the goal. If they move further forward, they can be bypassed by an attacker with the ball. Standing in the goal itself exposes it to being scored upon if, for example, while catching or rebounding, they pull their hands too far back, so that the ball is momentarily beyond the goal line. The goalkeeper has more opportunities to rest than the full-backs. Therefore, they should take the goal kick, either directly from the ground or (after being 'lifted' from the ground by a full-back) from the hands. The captain ('mother') is an experienced player who knows how to command the respect of others. They lead the entire team, assign positions to players, direct their preparation for matches, and ensure that they work well together. They also devise certain tricks and surprises for opponents and generally the tactical details most suited to the relative strengths of both teams. It is most beneficial for the team if the captain is in the second line of players (midfield); then they have the best view of the game, both during attack and defence. The captain carefully ensures that players do not sacrifice the good of their team for personal momentary pleasure. They reprimand unnecessary possession of the ball and dribbling it for too long; they reprimand showing off to the public with spectacular 'high kicks' and powerful shots when an unobtrusive short pass would often bring greater benefit. The referee also has a very difficult and responsible position. If possible, they should not belong to any of the playing teams and, in any case, must remember well that from the moment they assume the duties of referee, they stand above their personal feelings of friendship or dislike. Moreover, they should be chosen from among people who not only know the rules of the game perfectly but also all the details of their practical application, and even, which is particularly important, the more commonly used ways of circumventing or subtly infringing the rules. Keen eyesight, presence of mind, decisiveness, calmness, and tact—these are further qualities needed by a referee. The referee should run almost as much as a player. Their duty is to always be as close as possible to the ball to be able to judge the game themselves, not relying on the opinion of the assistant referees, let alone the players or spectators. When the ball approaches the penalty area, they must pay close attention to possible offside situations for the attackers. When shots on goal begin, their accurate assessment requires taking a position next to the goal. Showing even the slightest hesitation, the referee diminishes their authority. Their decisions must be quick, clear, and decisive. All unnecessary conversations and disputes with players, and especially with the public, are excluded. Only the referee's whistle can stop the game, and only with it can the ball be put back into play. The task of the two assistant referees is much simpler. Running (alongside the ball) on the touch lines, they signal (by raising the flag) to the referee when the ball goes out of play. In addition, the referee may ask for their opinion if they did not see some detail of the game. The assistant referees are most often provided one by each team. It is, of course, better if they are also taken from outside the competing sides.
