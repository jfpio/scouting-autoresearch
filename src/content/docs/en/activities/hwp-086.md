## Translated text

In a forest or terrain with clearings, many scattered trees, overgrown ground, thickets, hollows, etc.—where it is easy to hide and move unnoticed—the leader hides a letter, marking the hiding spot in a visible manner.

Then, he gathers both patrols, the whites and the reds, and after giving them the boundaries of the area within which the exercise will take place, he informs them in general terms about the letter’s hiding place. For example: *"There is a tree, tied with a white thread at waist height; 5 paces away from it, under a stone, lies the letter."* This is the letter the scouts must retrieve.

Both patrols then assemble at opposite ends of the terrain near a flag marking the camp location, and at a given signal, they set off to search for the letter.

The search must be conducted as inconspicuously as possible to observe the opponent’s movements without drawing their attention, and with great caution to avoid falling into an ambush. The terrain should be spacious to minimize encounters. However, since it is impossible to avoid them entirely when searching in different directions—and direct encounters may occur—the conditions for such meetings must be strictly defined (based on the general guidelines provided) and all participants must be familiarized with them before the exercise begins.

For example: if a scout’s armband (a colored thread) is torn off, he is taken prisoner and must, without hiding, go directly to the enemy’s camp. (Thus, anyone who does not feel strong enough to fight or sees the enemy’s advantage should step aside—or, if hidden and wishing to remain in place, stay silent without revealing their hiding spot.) If an encounter occurs at a distance of 15–20 paces, the smaller group must retreat before the opponent if the opponent demands it (though the opponent is not obligated to use this right and may instead attempt to surround the smaller group to take them prisoner).

The party that retrieves the letter wins. The opposing side can nullify this victory by capturing at least half of the patrol. However, before counting prisoners, an exchange must take place, as the opposing side may also have captives.

From the above description, one can imagine the course of the exercise. The patrol leader of the whites (or reds) divides the entire terrain among the scouts, providing them with methods of communication, such as signals for: *"Help!"*, *"I found it!"*, *"The enemy is on the letter’s trail!"*, *"The enemy has seized the letter!"*, etc. Each scout searches their assigned section, with scouts sometimes moving alone, sometimes in pairs, separating and then regrouping. Since retrieving the letter is far more important than capturing prisoners, encounters are generally avoided. The opponent is only forced to retreat or ambushed (possibly with called-for reinforcements) if they interfere too much and hinder the search. The reds do the same.

Eventually, someone discovers the letter’s hiding place. At that point, they either take it, avoiding all encounters and seeking support from their comrades, or, if the enemy’s presence prevents taking the letter, they notify their friends and, with their help, carry it away. Ostentatiously taking the letter may lead to its loss, as the enemy could tear off an armband, in which case the letter must be surrendered. A direct encounter may even occur near the hiding spot, and during the skirmish, someone might manage to snatch the letter and flee with it.

Even after taking the letter, the scouts must not destroy the marks made by the leader.
